<h2 id="short-answer">The Short Answer</h2>

<p>Which process should you fix first? <strong>The one where improving it saves or earns the most for the least effort.</strong> First, stop anything nobody uses. Then work out what each process costs a year in hours and dollars, plus any income it holds back, give it an ease score, multiply and start at the top. Not the one that annoys you most. Not the one with the coolest AI demo. The one with the biggest payback.</p>

<p>This follows on from <a href="/blog/your-real-job-in-finance-isnt-the-numbers-its-efficiency" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">why your real job in finance is effectiveness and efficiency</a>. Quick recap: effective means working on what moves the numbers that matter; efficient means doing it with the least time and money, every time. That post is the why. This one's the how.</p>

<h2 id="how-i-learned">How I Learned to Pick</h2>

<p>Early in my career I tried to fix everything at once. Early me also had a travel spreadsheet with 14 tabs for a four-day trip. Priorities weren't his strong suit. Here's what picking well has looked like since:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>RACQ:</strong> I built a Process Improvement Initiatives framework and dashboard that captured 20+ initiatives, each with an estimated return on investment. Management reviewed it every fortnight and went with the highest estimated ROI first. That's this whole post in one dashboard.</li>
  <li><strong>Flight Centre:</strong> I led the overhaul of accounts payable reporting to finance leaders and the CFO, covering over $100M a year. The size of that number is what made it effective to fix first.</li>
  <li><strong>Coronis:</strong> as the sole accountant supporting the CFO, I automated bulk journal imports and streamlined every process I inherited, freeing up more than 40% of my capacity for higher-value work.</li>
  <li><strong>Aveo:</strong> I cut manual accounts payable processing from 3 days to 1, part of redesigning manual data entry and reporting that removed about 30 hours of work a week.</li>
  <li><strong>yourtown:</strong> I redesigned the budget models for every service line, which cut the budget cycle by about 50% and added controls that reduced errors. Faster and more accurate: efficient and effective.</li>
</ul>

<p>None of those happened because I fixed everything. They happened because I picked the processes where better effectiveness and efficiency paid back most, fixed those properly, then moved on to the next.</p>

<h2 id="two-questions">How Effective? How Efficient?</h2>

<p>Every process on your list gets the same two questions:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>How effective is it?</strong> Who uses the output, and how big is the number it moves? A process that drives a big income or cost line is worth a lot. One nobody uses is worth nothing, however well it's done.</li>
  <li><strong>How efficient could it be?</strong> How much time, money and error is in it today, and how easily could you fix that?</li>
</ul>

<p>The best first fix is high on both: it matters a lot, and it's wasteful today. The scorecard below just puts numbers on those two questions.</p>

<h2 id="scorecard">The Scorecard, Step by Step</h2>

<p>There's plenty to fix. A 2026 study found <a href="https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2026/08/11/study-reveals-gap-between-the-ai-ambitions-of-accounting-teams-and-their-ability-to-execute/188332/" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">57% of month-end close cycles take seven days or more</a>, and that teams who set AI up properly are reclaiming around a quarter of their working time. Another put manual accounts payable at <a href="https://tipalti.com/global-finance-survey-2026/" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">an average of 10 hours a week</a>. Picking the right process first is what turns "we tried some AI" into hours and dollars you can point to, and it's exactly what leaders mean when a job ad asks for continuous improvement.</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Ask who cares.</strong> For each process, who uses the output and what decision does it change? If nobody can answer, stop it. That's the cheapest, fastest efficiency gain there is (I once found a team reconciling a bank account nobody had heard of).</li>
  <li><strong>List every recurring process.</strong> Brain-dump everything your team does daily, weekly, monthly and yearly. Bills, recs, reporting, payroll, expense claims, invoicing, chasing approvals.</li>
  <li><strong>Measure the time.</strong> Hours per run x runs per year = hours a year. Time it once with a stopwatch if you're guessing. Everyone underestimates.</li>
  <li><strong>Put a dollar figure on it.</strong> Hours a year x an hourly cost that includes super and on-costs. Then add the cost of what goes wrong: errors, rework, late fees, cash sitting idle. And if the process keeps income-earning staff stuck on admin, add the billable or care hours it costs you.</li>
  <li><strong>Score how easy it is to fix.</strong> 1 = a big system project, 5 = a rule, a template or an AI prompt could do it this week. AI has turned a lot of processes that used to be a 1 or 2 into a 4 or 5, so re-score anything you wrote off a year ago.</li>
  <li><strong>Multiply and rank.</strong> Value a year x ease = priority score. The biggest value is your effectiveness; the ease is how quickly you can make it efficient. Start at the top.</li>
  <li><strong>Re-score every quarter.</strong> Once you fix number one, everything moves up a spot.</li>
</ol>

<p>Notice the monthly reporting pack isn't first, even though it's probably the job everyone moans about. Bill entry quietly costs more, and it's easier to make efficient. That's exactly the kind of thing the scorecard catches and your gut misses. The value column is your effectiveness test. The ease column is your efficiency test.</p>

<p>This example only counts costs. If a process holds back income, like care staff stuck on paperwork instead of delivering care hours, or tradies chasing paperwork instead of billing, add that lost income too. At Aveo, lifting care-staff utilisation through better rostering and reporting meant more care hours from the same team, and every extra care hour was income.</p>

<p>Not sure what hourly cost to use? Take the salary, divide by about 1,976 working hours a year (38 hours x 52 weeks) and add about 25% for super, leave and on-costs. An $80,000 salary works out at roughly $50 an hour. It doesn't need to be perfect. It needs to be the same for every process so the ranking is fair.</p>

<h2 id="ai-fix">Where AI and AI Agents Fit</h2>

<p>Once you've picked number one, AI is usually the fastest way to fix it. It's new technology, and businesses will adopt it because it makes processes more efficient and cuts human error. Learning it on your own top process is the best way to get ahead of the curve.</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>High-volume, rule-based processes suit AI agents.</strong> Bill entry, bank recs, invoice reminders and expense coding can run on a schedule, with a person reviewing a short exceptions list.</li>
  <li><strong>Analysis and reporting suit Claude and other AI assistants.</strong> Building the reconciliation check, the reporting pack or the dashboard in hours instead of weeks.</li>
  <li><strong>Judgement-heavy work still needs you.</strong> Unusual transactions, approvals, and anything that moves money or goes to the ATO.</li>
</ul>

<p>In the example, fixing bill entry might mean an AI agent reads bills from the accounts inbox and drafts them in Xero, with a person reviewing and approving (I tested exactly this in <a href="/blog/your-bookkeepers-on-holiday-your-ai-agents-arent-xero-over-the-christmas-shutdown" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">AI agents running Xero over the Christmas shutdown</a>). If that cuts 6 hours a week down to 1 hour of review, that's 260 hours, about $13,000 a year, back in the team's pocket, with fewer keying errors. Then bank recs become number one, and you go again.</p>

<p>But remember: AI makes a process faster. It doesn't make it matter. That's why the scorecard starts with "who cares?" and the value of the process, not with the tool.</p>

<h2 id="traps">Four Traps to Avoid</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Fixing the fun one.</strong> The process with the coolest automation isn't always the most effective one to fix.</li>
  <li><strong>Fixing the rare one.</strong> A painful once-a-year job usually loses to a mildly annoying weekly one. Frequency is where the hours hide.</li>
  <li><strong>Automating a broken process.</strong> Simplify first. Automating a bad process just gets you bad results faster.</li>
  <li><strong>Automating without understanding the impact.</strong> An AI agent will happily reconcile a bank account nobody needs, faster and more accurately than ever. That's an efficient waste. Know what the process does for the business before you automate it.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="your-prompts">Score Your Own Responsibilities</h2>

<p>This prompt for Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini lists every process in your responsibilities, scores each one exactly like the worked example above, and ranks them. Because priorities change as you fix things, it finishes by offering to re-score your list every quarter on a schedule, so you don't have to remember.</p>

  <p class="paul-prompt">You're my process improvement coach. Help me score every process I'm responsible for and decide which to fix first.

Step 1: Interview me to list every process in my responsibilities. Ask me to brain-dump everything I do daily, weekly, monthly, quarterly and yearly, then ask follow-ups until we've got them all.

Step 2: For each process, ask me (one process at a time, keep it quick):
- How often it runs and how long each run takes
- Who uses the output and what decision it changes
- What goes wrong when it's late or wrong (errors, rework, late fees) and a rough yearly cost

Step 3: Ask me for my hourly cost, or estimate it from my salary plus about 25% for super and on-costs.

Step 4: Score every process like this:
- Cost a year = (hours a year x hourly cost) + error and delay costs + income held back
- Ease to fix, 1 to 5 (1 = big system project, 5 = a rule, template or AI prompt could fix it this week), with one line on why

Step 5: Show me the ranked list, highest score first, in this format for each process:
#1 Process name

Ease (1-5): [score]
Score: [score]

Step 7: For my top 3, tell me the first practical step to fix each, whether Claude or an AI agent could run it (and on what schedule), what I must still check, and a realistic estimate of hours saved and errors avoided.

Step 8: Ask me if I want this re-scored on a schedule (e.g. the first Monday of each quarter). If yes, save my list and answers so future runs only ask what's changed. Set up the scheduled task if you can; if you can't, tell me the simplest way to schedule it with the tools I have.
