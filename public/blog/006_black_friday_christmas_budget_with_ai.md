<p>Black Friday 2026 in Australia is <strong>Friday 27 November</strong>, with Cyber Monday on 30 November. The smartest thing you can do before then is set a Christmas budget, and AI makes that a 15-minute job: get your actual spending numbers, let ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude interview you, then stick to the number it helps you land on.</p>

<p>This is general education, not personal financial advice.</p>

<h2 id="when-is-black-friday">When Is Black Friday 2026 in Australia?</h2>

<p>Black Friday is the day after US Thanksgiving, which falls on the fourth Thursday of November. In 2026 that makes it <strong>Friday 27 November</strong>, followed by Cyber Monday on Monday 30 November.</p>

<p>In practice, Australian retailers don't wait for the actual day. Sales tend to stretch across the whole week (sometimes longer), and the "early access" emails start well before. Consider it the opening ceremony of the Annual Inbox Siege. So if you want a budget in place before the first "don't miss out" email, this week is the week.</p>

<h2 id="two-kinds-of-sales-worry">Two Kinds of Sales Worry</h2>

<p>As a business coach, I hear a lot of worry this time of year. For many business owners it's the quiet period: customers head off on holidays and sales dip.</p>

<p>For everyone else, it's the opposite problem. Not a drought of sales, a flood of them. Every inbox, app and billboard is yelling "40% off", while you're also trying to buy for the whole family, the in-laws and the Secret Santa you drew at work.</p>

<p>Different worry, same cure. <strong>Once you've got a budget, the anxiety drops</strong>, because you're not making decisions at the checkout anymore. You already made them.</p>

<h2 id="why-this-year">Why This Christmas Feels Tighter</h2>

<p>On Melbourne Cup Day the Reserve Bank lifted the cash rate to 4.85%, the fifth rise of 2026. <a href="https://www.savings.com.au/news/rba-lifts-cash-rate-to-4-85" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">Savings.com.au estimates</a> that could add around $80 a month to repayments on a $500,000 mortgage, though it varies by loan. Not exactly the Cup Day result anyone was hoping for.</p>

<p>Meanwhile, Pureprofile research of 1,015 Australians, <a href="https://itbrief.com.au/story/australians-cut-christmas-spending-as-cost-pressures-rise" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">reported by IT Brief</a>, found 58% plan to use sales events to help cover Christmas, 47% of Black Friday shoppers often spend more than planned, and 44% feel pressured by the promotions to spend money they didn't intend to.</p>

<p>So more than half of us use the sales to save money, and nearly half of us blow the budget in them. In accounting we'd call that a variance. And <strong>you can't have a variance without a budget</strong> to measure it against.</p>

<h2 id="get-your-actuals">Step 1: Get Your Actual Spending</h2>

<p>Here's the bit most budgeting advice skips. A budget built on guesses is just a wish list with numbers on it. AI can't budget with numbers it doesn't have, so before you open ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude, you need to know what you <strong>actually spend</strong> each month. Pick whichever option suits you.</p>

<h3>Option 1: Screenshot your banking app</h3>

<p>Many banking apps already sort your spending into categories like groceries, transport, eating out and shopping. Open the spending summary for the last three months and take a screenshot. Before you share it, crop out your name, account numbers and anything else that identifies you.</p>

<p>ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude can all read images, so you can paste the screenshot straight in and ask: "Turn this into a table of my average monthly spending by category." Done in a minute.</p>

<h3>Option 2: Use a budgeting app</h3>

<p>If your bank's categories are a bit creative (why is Bunnings under "entertainment"?), a dedicated budgeting app does a better job. <a href="https://www.pocketsmith.com/" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">PocketSmith</a>, for example, connects to your bank accounts and tracks where your money goes, with reports by category. Once it's set up, your category totals are always there, ready to hand to an AI.</p>

<h3>Option 3: Let AI sort your transactions</h3>

<p>Want the full picture? Export your transactions from internet banking as a CSV file. Delete the columns with your name, account number and BSB, keeping just the date, description and amount. Then upload it and ask the AI to categorise every transaction and total each category by month.</p>

<p>That used to be a PivotTable job in Excel (and back at Flight Centre I was the person people came to for exactly that). I also keep a spreadsheet for my travel plans, so trust me, I understand the urge to do it all by hand. Resist it. Now AI can do the sorting for you. You just check the categories look right.</p>

<h3>What to share with AI (and what not to)</h3>

<p><a href="https://www.moneymag.com.au/mel-robbins-ai-money-tip-risk" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">Money magazine</a> notes the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner advises against entering personal and sensitive information into AI tools. So whichever option you pick, the rule is simple: <strong>give AI the shape of your money, not the details of it</strong>. Totals and categories, yes. Account numbers, BSBs, full names and logins, never.</p>

<h2 id="let-ai-interview-you">Step 2: Let AI Build Your Christmas Budget</h2>

<p>Here's the trick that makes this work. Instead of dumping everything into one giant message, <strong>get the AI to interview you, one question at a time</strong>. It's like sitting down with someone who asks good questions, and it means nothing gets missed (like the cousin you forgot you drew in Kris Kringle).</p>

<p>Hit the copy button below, paste it into ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude, and answer as it goes.</p>

<blockquote class="paul-prompt-box" style="background: #f9fafb; border: 1px solid #e5e7eb; border-radius: 12px; padding: 2rem; margin: 2rem 0; color: #1F3A7D; border-left: 4px solid #00FF00;">
<strong>📋 PAUL's Prompt (Christmas Budget Interview)</strong>
<p>Copy it, paste it into your AI tool, then answer its questions with your rounded category totals.</p>

<details style="background: #ffffff; border: 1px solid #e5e7eb; border-radius: 8px; padding: 1.5rem; margin: 1rem 0;">
  <summary style="cursor: pointer; font-weight: 700; color: #1f3a7d;">📋 See Full Prompt (Click to Expand)</summary>
  <p class="paul-prompt">I want you to help me build a Black Friday and Christmas budget. I'm in Australia and all amounts are in AUD.

Interview me ONE question at a time. Wait for my answer before asking the next question. I'll give you rounded numbers or a cropped screenshot, so never ask for bank statements, account numbers, BSBs or personal details.

Cover these, in this order:
1. My monthly take-home pay.
2. My fixed bills each month (rent or mortgage, utilities, insurance, phone, transport).
3. My actual everyday spending by category for the last three months (I'll paste totals or a screenshot).
4. How much I already have set aside for Christmas.
5. Who I'm buying gifts for (I'll use labels like "Mum" or "Work Secret Santa", not full names) and any group spending limits.
6. Whether I'm hosting or travelling, and roughly what that costs.
7. Anything I genuinely need that I'm hoping to buy in the Black Friday sales.

When you have everything:
- Show me how much I can spend on Christmas without using credit, and show your working.
- Split it into gifts, food and drink, hosting or travel, a Black Friday "needs list" and a 5 to 10% buffer.
- Give me a per-person gift limit.
- Build a tracker table (Item | Category | Budget | Actual | Difference) I can keep updating.
- If my numbers don't add up, tell me plainly. Don't force a budget to fit.</p>
</details>

<p>That last line matters more than it looks. AI tools are people-pleasers. Left alone, they'll make the numbers work even when they shouldn't. Giving it permission to say "this doesn't add up" turns it from a yes-man into a useful sounding board.</p>

<h2 id="survive-black-friday">Step 3: Survive Black Friday Week</h2>

<p>Once your budget exists, a few simple habits keep it intact through the Black Friday sales (advice I'm giving myself too, as a new dad who has just discovered how many "essential" baby gadgets go on sale):</p>

<ul>
  <li>Write your list before 27 November. If it's not on the list, it's not a deal. It's a new purchase with a red sticker on it.</li>
  <li><strong>Set one dollar cap for the whole sales period.</strong> One number is much easier to stick to than ten.</li>
  <li>Wait 24 hours on anything that wasn't on the list. Funny how often "ends tonight" is still on the next day.</li>
  <li>Mute the sale emails for the week if they get to you.</li>
  <li>Log each purchase the same day. Thirty seconds now beats a January jump scare.</li>
</ul>

<p>And if you're about to cave, ask your AI: "I'm about to buy this for this price. It wasn't on my list. Ask me three blunt questions to check if I actually need it." It's like having a mate tap you on the shoulder at the checkout.</p>

<h2 id="check-the-maths">Check the Maths Before You Trust It</h2>

<p>I started my career in audit at PwC, and the one habit audit drills into you is that nothing gets signed off until someone has checked it. Treat your AI budget the same way: <strong>the AI writes the draft, you're the reviewer</strong>.</p>

<ul>
  <li>Add the categories up yourself. AI occasionally gives you a table where the total doesn't match the rows, with complete confidence.</li>
  <li>Make sure there's a buffer. A budget that only works if nothing goes wrong in December isn't a budget.</li>
  <li>If it suggests a credit card, payment plan or investment, that's not a recommendation. That's your cue to speak to a licensed professional.</li>
</ul>

<p>The maths is rarely the hard part. As I wrote in <a href="/blog/finance-coaching-why-smart-people-struggle-with-money" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">why smart people struggle with money</a>, even accountants can't always tell you where their own money goes. What helps is a simple system you'll actually use when the pressure's on, and late November is when the pressure's on.</p>
