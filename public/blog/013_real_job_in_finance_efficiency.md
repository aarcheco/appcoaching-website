<p>What's your real job in finance? It isn't to make the numbers balance. It's to help the organisation keep more money than it spends, by making it <strong>more effective and more efficient</strong>. That's true for a listed company, a family business and, yes, a not-for-profit. The numbers are the scoreboard. Your job is to help win the game.</p>

<h2 id="the-real-job">The Real Job (It's Not the Numbers)</h2>

<p>As a grad, I genuinely thought my job was to make things balance. Tick, flick, reconcile, done. It took me an embarrassingly long time to realise the business didn't much care if the numbers balanced if they balanced badly.</p>

<p>At PwC I audited big clients across mining, retail, financial services and not-for-profit. Wildly different businesses, same underlying goal: widen the gap between money coming in and money going out, and do it without falling over.</p>

<p>At Flight Centre, store leaders would ask me, "why does my P&amp;L look like this?" The useful answer was never a debit and a credit. It was which levers moved their profit: sales, margins, staff hours and costs. Once they saw that, they changed how they ran the store. That's finance doing its real job.</p>

<p>Nobody wins a grand final by staring at the scoreboard. (Everton fans have had a lot of practice at this.)</p>

<h2 id="even-nfps">Even Not-for-Profits Want a Profit</h2>

<p>Here's the bit that surprises most young finance pros. Not-for-profits want a profit too. They just call it a surplus.</p>

<p>I've worked at yourtown, the charity behind Kids Helpline. A charity with a big heart and a serious finance function, because every dollar of surplus means more counsellors answering more calls from kids. Kids Helpline was <a href="https://www.yourtown.com.au/sites/default/files/document/Kids-Helpline-Insights-2020-Historical-Report-Final.pdf" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">launched in 1991 entirely self-funded by BoysTown lotteries</a>, and yourtown's CEO has said the service is <a href="https://www.yourtown.com.au/node/1633" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">more than 70% funded by the community, mostly through Prize Home ticket sales</a>. A charity selling multi-million-dollar prize homes to fund counselling. If that isn't commercial thinking, I don't know what is.</p>

<p>The regulator agrees. The ACNC says <a href="https://www.acnc.gov.au/node/983" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">a charity can make a surplus as long as it's used for its charitable purposes, and that generating one is generally considered good practice</a>. Not-for-profit describes where the profit goes, not whether there is one. Nobody gets a dividend. Especially not the finance team.</p>

<h2 id="effective-efficient">Effective vs Efficient</h2>

<p>Two words that sound like corporate bingo but are worth getting straight:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Effective</strong> means doing the right things: the work that drives revenue, the mission or cash.</li>
  <li><strong>Efficient</strong> means doing those things with the least time and money.</li>
</ul>

<p>A finance team that produces a beautiful 40-page monthly pack nobody reads is efficient at being ineffective. A team that gives the CEO three numbers that change a decision, in an hour instead of a week, is both. Every process you touch either helps the business earn, save or protect cash, or it's overhead. Your job is to grow the first group and shrink the second.</p>

<h2 id="why-now">Why This Matters More Than Ever</h2>

<p>Finance teams are drowning in manual work. One survey found <a href="https://www.corpay.com/resources/blog/where-finance-time-disappears" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">86% of finance teams spend six or more hours per person, per week on admin</a> across expenses, invoices and supplier payments. Another found <a href="https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2026/08/11/study-reveals-gap-between-the-ai-ambitions-of-accounting-teams-and-their-ability-to-execute/188332/" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">six in ten accountants spend 40% or more of their time on reconciliations, data entry and other busy work</a>.</p>

<p>That's the opportunity. The fastest way for a young finance pro to add value isn't a fancier report. It's giving people their hours back. And with AI doing more of the manual work, the finance people who understand how the organisation makes money, and point the tools at the right problems, are the ones who get noticed.</p>

<h2 id="first-year">What This Looks Like in Your First Year</h2>

<p>You don't need a fancy title to think this way. A few examples of the same task, done two ways:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Month-end reporting.</strong> The grad version rebuilds last month's report with new numbers. The commercial version asks who reads it and what decision it changes, then cuts the pages nobody uses.</li>
  <li><strong>Paying suppliers.</strong> The grad version processes the bills. The commercial version notices the business keeps paying late fees, works out what they cost a year and fixes the approval bottleneck causing them.</li>
  <li><strong>Reconciliations.</strong> The grad version ticks and flicks. The commercial version spots that the same three suppliers cause most of the unmatched items, and fixes it at the source.</li>
</ul>

<p>Same job title. Very different value. One version gets you through the month. The other gets you a reputation as the person who makes things better, and that's the version that gets promoted.</p>

<h2 id="think-like-this">How to Think Like This From Day One</h2>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Learn how your organisation makes money.</strong> Or for a not-for-profit, where the funding comes from and what a surplus pays for. Ask. Nobody minds a grad asking that.</li>
  <li><strong>Trace every task to a lever.</strong> Does it affect revenue, cost, cash or risk? If the answer is "none", ask why you're doing it.</li>
  <li><strong>Find the biggest time sinks.</strong> The manual, repetitive jobs that eat hours every week.</li>
  <li><strong>Talk in hours and dollars.</strong> "This takes 6 hours a week, about $15,000 a year" gets attention. "This is a bit annoying" doesn't.</li>
  <li><strong>Fix one thing properly.</strong> Then do the next one. My next post covers how to pick which process to fix first.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="your-prompt">Find Where Your Role Adds Value</h2>

<p>This prompt interviews you about your organisation and your week, then maps every task to revenue, cost, cash or nothing. It's a slightly confronting exercise. Mine would have shown a lot of very well-formatted nothing in my grad year.</p>

<p>Paste it into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini and answer its questions honestly.</p>

<details style="background: #ffffff; border: 1px solid #e5e7eb; border-radius: 8px; padding: 1.5rem; margin: 1rem 0;">
  <summary style="cursor: pointer; font-weight: 700; color: #1f3a7d;">📋 See Full Prompt (Click to Expand)</summary>
  <p class="paul-prompt">You're an experienced finance business partner helping me understand how my role adds (or wastes) value for my organisation.

First, interview me one question at a time:
1. What does my organisation do, and how does it make money? (If it's a not-for-profit: where does its funding come from, and what does a surplus pay for?)
2. What's my role and team?
3. List every task I do in a typical week and month, roughly how long each takes, and who uses the output.

Then build me a table with these columns: task, hours per month, which lever it affects (revenue, cost, cash, risk or none), how directly (direct, indirect, none), and one line on why.

After the table:
1. Name my 3 highest-value tasks: the ones closest to profit or surplus.
2. Name my 3 lowest-value time sinks, with the hours per month they cost.
3. For each time sink, suggest a fix (automate, simplify, stop or hand off) and the tool or AI approach that could do it.
4. Write 3 questions I could ask my manager to understand what the business needs most from finance right now.

Keep it plain English. No jargon. Be honest if a task adds nothing.</p>
</details>
