<p>What's your real job in finance? It isn't to make the numbers balance. It's to help the organisation be <strong>more effective and more efficient</strong>, so it keeps more money than it spends. Effective means working on what moves the numbers that matter. Efficient means doing that with the least time and money, again and again. The numbers are the scoreboard. Your job is to help win the game.</p>

<h2 id="two-words">The Two Words Leaders Care About</h2>

<p>Every leader I've worked for, at PwC clients, Flight Centre, a charity and smaller businesses, cared about the same two things:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Effectiveness</strong> is impact. Is this work moving a big line on the P&amp;L or balance sheet, or nothing at all?</li>
  <li><strong>Efficiency</strong> is doing that work with the least time and money, so the business can hit maximum effectiveness every week, not just once.</li>
</ul>

<p>That's why "continuous improvement" turns up in almost every finance job ad. Leaders need people in the engine room who keep making the business more effective and more efficient, not people who produce the same report the same slow way forever.</p>

<p>As a grad, I thought my job was to make things balance. It took me an embarrassingly long time to realise the business didn't much care if the numbers balanced if they balanced badly.</p>

<h2 id="start-with-pl">Start With the P&amp;L, Not the Process</h2>

<p>The fastest way to know what's effective is to start with the financials and work backwards. Every big line on the P&amp;L has drivers:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Income drivers:</strong> sales volume, price, billable or care hours, how many staff are actually earning revenue.</li>
  <li><strong>Cost drivers:</strong> staff hours and wages, suppliers, rework, late fees, systems.</li>
</ul>

<p>Behind each driver sit the operational, sales and staffing processes and the KPIs that measure them. Rank them by the size of the line they move. The processes behind the biggest lines are where effectiveness lives, so they're the ones worth making efficient first. The ones that move very little, or nothing, are the ones to simplify or stop.</p>

<p>At Flight Centre, store leaders would ask me, "why does my P&amp;L look like this?" The useful answer was never a debit and a credit. It was which drivers moved their profit. Once they saw that, they changed how they ran the store. Nobody wins a grand final by staring at the scoreboard. (Everton fans have had plenty of practice.)</p>

<h2 id="who-cares">Ask: Who Cares About This Process?</h2>

<p>Before you make a process efficient, ask one blunt question: who uses this, and what decision does it change?</p>

<p>At Flight Centre I found a team in the marketing division doing a monthly bank reconciliation. I asked who looked at the bank account. They named a particular executive. So I asked the executive. They'd never heard of it. A team was spending its time every month carefully reconciling a bank account that should have been closed years earlier.</p>

<p>Beautifully reconciled, to the cent, for nobody. Peak efficiency, zero effectiveness. And big organisations are full of these: reports with no reader, approvals nobody remembers adding, outputs an executive has never seen. Every one costs labour hours and wages, which comes straight off the bottom line.</p>

<h2 id="income">Efficiency Grows Income, Not Just Savings</h2>

<p>Most people think efficiency only means cutting costs. It's just as much about earning more.</p>

<p>At Aveo, the income came from care staff delivering care hours. So the question was: how efficiently can we deliver care, and how many more care hours can the same team deliver? We tracked utilisation (care hours delivered against hours paid), made it visible to managers so they could act on it, and fixed rostering and scheduling so more of each shift went to actual care. More care delivered, more income, from the same people.</p>

<p>It's the same in trades and construction. If the processes around a tradie are slow, with quoting, job paperwork and chasing materials, they bill fewer hours. Fix the process and utilisation goes up, and so does revenue. That's effectiveness and efficiency working together.</p>

<h2 id="costs">Efficiency Stops the Bleed</h2>

<p>On the cost side, the waste is usually hiding in plain sight. At Coronis I inherited the classics: reporting rebuilt by hand every period, the same data typed into more than one system, approvals that sat waiting for days, reports nobody read, and paper processes that should have been digital years ago. Streamlining and automating them freed up more than 40% of my capacity.</p>

<p>None of those processes was a disaster on its own. Together, they were hours of wages every week spent on work with little or no impact. That's the bottom line leaking out one task at a time.</p>

<h2 id="excel-to-ai">From Excel to AI: Same Job, Faster</h2>

<p>For most of my career, my efficiency engine was Excel. Pivot tables, Power Query and built-in checks and balances made my journals and reports faster, more accurate and easier for the next person to run. It's how I ended up as <a href="/blog/25-common-excel-mistakes-i-fixed-for-years-and-how-i-became-mr-excel-at-flight-centre" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">"Mr Excel" at Flight Centre</a>.</p>

<p>AI does that same job, and a lot more:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Spreadsheets:</strong> formulas, data cleaning and reconciliation checks in minutes instead of hours.</li>
  <li><strong>Emails:</strong> the follow-ups, explanations and chasers drafted in seconds.</li>
  <li><strong>Reports and dashboards:</strong> a new one built in an afternoon, not a month. I wrote about <a href="/blog/power-bi-took-me-months-claude-and-supabase-took-minutes" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">a dashboard that took me months in Power BI and minutes with AI</a>.</li>
  <li><strong>Insight:</strong> the "so what" in front of executives faster, so they make effective decisions faster.</li>
</ul>

<p>That last one is the big shift. Efficiency used to mean saving finance's own time. With AI, it means speeding up the whole business's decisions, and a good decision made this week beats a perfect report delivered three weeks late. That's effectiveness and efficiency at the same time. Excel and I had a great run, and we're still friends. I'm just seeing other tools now.</p>

<h2 id="even-nfps">Even Not-for-Profits Run on This</h2>

<p>Here's the bit that surprises most young finance pros: not-for-profits care about effectiveness and efficiency just as much. They want a profit too. They just call it a surplus.</p>

<p>I've worked at yourtown, the charity behind Kids Helpline. There, effectiveness is measured in impact: every dollar of surplus means more counsellors answering more calls from kids. Efficiency means more of every dollar reaches those kids instead of being lost in the back office. And the money has always been earned commercially. Kids Helpline was <a href="https://www.yourtown.com.au/sites/default/files/document/Kids-Helpline-Insights-2020-Historical-Report-Final.pdf" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">launched in 1991 entirely self-funded by BoysTown lotteries</a>, and yourtown's CEO has said it's <a href="https://www.yourtown.com.au/node/1633" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">more than 70% funded by the community, mostly through Prize Home ticket sales</a>.</p>

<p>The regulator agrees. The ACNC says <a href="https://www.acnc.gov.au/node/983" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">a charity can make a surplus as long as it's used for its charitable purposes, and that generating one is generally considered good practice</a>. Not-for-profit describes where the profit goes, not whether there is one. Nobody gets a dividend. Especially not the finance team.</p>

<h2 id="day-one">How to Think Like This From Day One</h2>

<p>There's no shortage of waste to find. One survey found <a href="https://www.corpay.com/resources/blog/where-finance-time-disappears" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">86% of finance teams spend six or more hours per person, per week on admin</a>, and another found <a href="https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2026/08/11/study-reveals-gap-between-the-ai-ambitions-of-accounting-teams-and-their-ability-to-execute/188332/" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">six in ten accountants spend 40% or more of their time on reconciliations, data entry and other busy work</a>. Here's how to start:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Learn the drivers.</strong> What moves the biggest income and cost lines? Ask. Nobody minds a grad asking that.</li>
  <li><strong>Ask who cares.</strong> For every process you touch: who uses the output, and what decision does it change? No answer, no process.</li>
  <li><strong>Rate it twice.</strong> How effective is it (size of impact) and how efficient is it (time and money it takes)?</li>
  <li><strong>Let AI do the heavy lifting.</strong> Use it to build the check, the report or the email faster, so your time goes on the insight and the decision.</li>
  <li><strong>Talk in hours and dollars.</strong> "This takes 6 hours a week, about $15,000 a year, and nobody uses it" gets attention.</li>
  <li><strong>Improve one thing, then the next.</strong> That's continuous improvement. My next post covers <a href="/blog/which-process-should-you-fix-first-follow-the-time-and-the-money" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">how to pick which process to fix first</a>.</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="your-prompt">Find Where Your Work Has Impact</h2>

<p>This prompt interviews you about your organisation's drivers and your week, then rates every task for effectiveness and efficiency. It's a slightly confronting exercise. Mine would have shown a lot of very well-formatted nothing in my grad year.</p>

<blockquote class="paul-prompt-box" style="background: #f9fafb; border: 1px solid #e5e7eb; border-radius: 12px; padding: 2rem; margin: 2rem 0; color: #1F3A7D; border-left: 4px solid #00FF00;">
<strong>📋 PAUL's Prompt (Profit Line-of-Sight)</strong>
<p>Paste it into ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini and answer its questions honestly.</p>

<details style="background: #ffffff; border: 1px solid #e5e7eb; border-radius: 8px; padding: 1.5rem; margin: 1rem 0;">
  <summary style="cursor: pointer; font-weight: 700; color: #1f3a7d;">📋 See Full Prompt (Click to Expand)</summary>
  <p class="paul-prompt">You're an experienced finance business partner. Help me see which parts of my work are effective (they move the numbers that matter) and which are just efficient at being pointless.

First, interview me one question at a time:
1. What does my organisation do, and how does it make money? (If it's a not-for-profit: where does its funding come from, and what does a surplus pay for?)
2. What are the biggest income and cost lines on our P&L, and what drives them (e.g. sales volume, price, billable or care hours, staff hours, supplier costs)?
3. What's my role and team?
4. List every task I do in a typical week and month, roughly how long each takes, and who uses the output.

Then build me a table with these columns: task, hours per month, who uses the output and for what decision, which P&L or balance sheet line it affects, size of impact (high, medium, low, none), and how efficiently it's done now (good, could be better, wasteful).

After the table:
1. My 3 most effective tasks: the ones that move the biggest lines.
2. My 3 biggest wastes: tasks nobody uses, or that take far more time than their impact deserves, with the hours per month they cost.
3. Any task where nobody can name who uses the output. Tell me to ask that question before I do it again.
4. For each waste, suggest a fix (stop, simplify, automate or hand off) and the tool or AI approach that could do it.
5. 3 questions I could ask my manager to find out which numbers they care about most right now.

Keep it plain English. Be honest if a task adds nothing.</p>
</details>
