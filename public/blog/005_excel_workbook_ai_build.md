<h2 id="the-message">The LinkedIn Message</h2>

<p>I never worked directly with this guy at Flight Centre. He was in a different team, someone I'd cross paths with in passing — approachable, easy to talk to, the kind of person people just liked having around. I moved on. He moved on. Years went by. Different companies, different industries, no contact.</p>

<p>Then one day I'm scrolling Airtasker, of all places, and I apply for a job someone's posted. The client messages back: "Wait, is this Aaron? From Flight Centre?" Turns out he still remembered me — specifically, he remembered me as the person who was good with Excel. Somehow that stuck for a decade, longer than most of the actual work details from that job. He'd since moved into a procurement manager role, wanted to sharpen his Excel skills properly, and figured he'd ask.</p>

<p>That's the whole story. No pitch, no marketing, no funnel. Just someone remembering that I was approachable and genuinely useful at a specific skill, years after the fact.</p>

<h2 id="networking-still-wins">Why Networking Still Wins in an AI World</h2>

<p>It's easy to assume that in an AI-driven job market, technical skills are the only currency that matters. This story says otherwise. What got remembered wasn't a job title or a line on a CV — it was that I was easy to approach and that I could actually explain what I knew in a way someone else could use.</p>

<p>That combination — real, teachable skill plus being someone people feel comfortable reaching back out to — compounds over years in a way most career advice underrates. AI is genuinely changing what skills are worth building and how fast you can apply them (more on that below), but it hasn't changed the fact that people hire, refer, and recommend other people they remember positively. Early in your career, that's often worth more than the extra certification.</p>

<p>The teaching part matters just as much as the knowing part. Knowing Excel is one skill. Being able to explain a VLOOKUP to someone who's never built a formula before, without making them feel stupid, is a different skill — and it's the one that gets you remembered a decade later.</p>

<p>As we went, the brief grew: procurement-themed practice datasets so the examples felt relevant to his actual job, dozens of individually explained formulas, self-marking practice exercises, three full 50-question exams, and the whole thing dressed in AppCoaching.io's own brand colours and logo. Nothing about that scope was small. It just wasn't going to get built with me sitting in Excel typing formulas into 33 tabs one at a time.</p>

<h2 id="building-with-claude">Building It With Claude Instead of By Hand</h2>

<p>The workbook was never typed into Excel by hand. It was generated by a set of Python scripts that Claude wrote, ran, and refined — the whole file rebuilt from code, not assembled cell-by-cell in the Excel UI. The pipeline settled into a clear order:</p>

<p><strong>1. Clarifying interview.</strong> Before building anything, Claude asked about quiz format, the shape of the PowerQuery dataset, and how much scaffolding a true beginner would need.</p>

<p><strong>2. Skeleton build.</strong> One script per major section — datasets, formula-teaching tabs, quizzes, Section 2 features, Section 3's relational tables and reporting tools.</p>

<p><strong>3. A shared style library.</strong> Fonts, colours, borders, and layout helpers — title bars, section headers, "worked example" and "your answer" cell styling — centralised in one place so every one of the 33 tabs looked consistent.</p>

<p><strong>4. A shared quiz engine.</strong> One reusable function built the "Your Answer → Correct Formula → Correct Answer → Check" mechanic used everywhere: every practice task, both Knowledge Checks, and all three exams. Built once, reused dozens of times.</p>

<p><strong>5. A recalculation quality gate.</strong> After every significant change, the whole workbook got rebuilt from scratch and run through a formula-recalculation tool. Every flagged error got individually inspected — most were harmless (question text that happened to contain the word "#N/A"), but several were real bugs: a broken cross-sheet reference, a <code>#DIV/0!</code> from a zero-match <code>AVERAGEIFS</code>, an unquoted sheet name with a space in it. The kind of thing that's easy to miss eyeballing a spreadsheet, but impossible to miss when a script actually recalculates every formula and reports what broke.</p>

<p><strong>6. Multiple full revision passes</strong>, each triggered by feedback — consolidating 16 separate formula tabs down to 6, rewriting multiple-choice questions to use safe dropdowns instead of free text, correcting the brand accent colour after the client flagged that the real logo uses green rather than gold (verified by sampling the actual logo file's pixels in Python, not eyeballing a screenshot), and adding a full "Excel Fundamentals" tab after an audit turned up a gap in the foundations.</p>

<p>One detail worth sitting with: when I needed to guarantee that no two "Simple Formulas" practice questions produced the same numeric answer, Claude reproduced the practice dataset in Python and computed every candidate answer to hand-pick combinations that were provably distinct. That's not a formatting trick — that's actual computation replacing what would otherwise be trial and error in Excel itself.</p>

<h2 id="the-catch">The Catch: You Still Need to Know Excel</h2>

<p>Here's the part I don't want glossed over. None of this works if the person driving it doesn't actually know Excel. Claude wrote the scripts, generated the formulas, and rebuilt the file a dozen times in one sitting — but I still had to review every flagged error from the recalculation gate and judge which ones were real bugs versus false alarms. I still had to know that an <code>AVERAGEIFS</code> returning <code>#DIV/0!</code> on a zero-match condition is expected behaviour, not a formula written wrong. I still had to know what "good" looks like in a self-marking quiz mechanic, in a PivotTable, in a Power Query step — because Claude can build fast, but it can't tell you whether what it built is actually teaching the right thing.</p>

<p>This is the same principle behind <a href="/blog/how-to-build-a-website-with-ai-no-code-under-100" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">building a website with AI</a>: the tool collapses the time it takes to produce something, it doesn't collapse the expertise needed to know if the output is right. If you can't read a formula, you can't catch the one formula in 2,126 that's subtly wrong. AI is a force multiplier on existing skill, not a replacement for it.</p>

<h2 id="what-this-means">What This Means for You</h2>

<p>If you're early in your career: the technical skills you build matter, but so does being someone people feel comfortable reaching back out to years later. Being approachable and being able to actually teach what you know is its own compounding asset. Nobody remembered a job title a decade on — they remembered being helped by someone who was easy to talk to.</p>

<p>If you're building anything complex with AI — a training resource, a model, a course, a workbook — the lesson is the same one from this whole project: AI collapses build time and makes broad, consistent changes trivial, but it doesn't remove the need for real expertise reviewing the output. Use it to move faster. Don't use it as a substitute for knowing your subject.</p>
