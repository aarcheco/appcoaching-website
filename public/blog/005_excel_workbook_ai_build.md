<h2 id="the-message">The LinkedIn Message</h2>

<p>I never worked directly with this guy at Flight Centre. He was in a different team, someone I'd cross paths with in passing — approachable, easy to talk to, the kind of person people just liked having around. I moved on. He moved on. Years went by. Different companies, different industries, no contact.</p>

<p>Then one day I'm scrolling Airtasker, of all places, and I apply for a job someone's posted. The client messages back: "Wait, is this Aaron? From Flight Centre?" Turns out he still remembered me — specifically, he remembered me as the person who was good with Excel. Somehow that stuck for a decade, longer than most of the actual work details from that job (a career legacy, of sorts). He'd since moved into a procurement manager role, wanted to sharpen his Excel skills properly, and figured he'd ask.</p>

<p>That's the whole story. No pitch, no marketing, no funnel. My entire sales strategy, it turns out, was being nice to people and being good at spreadsheets. Just someone remembering that I was approachable and genuinely useful at a specific skill, years after the fact.</p>

<h2 id="networking-still-wins">Why Networking Still Wins in an AI World</h2>

<p>It's easy to assume that in an AI-driven job market, technical skills are the only currency that matters. This story says otherwise. What got remembered wasn't a job title or a line on a CV — it was that I was easy to approach and that I could actually explain what I knew in a way someone else could use.</p>

<p>That combination — real, teachable skill plus being someone people feel comfortable reaching back out to — compounds over years in a way most career advice underrates. AI is genuinely changing what skills are worth building and how fast you can apply them (more on that below), but it hasn't changed the fact that people hire, refer, and recommend other people they remember positively. Early in your career, that's often worth more than the extra certification.</p>

<p>The teaching part matters just as much as the knowing part. Knowing Excel is one skill. Being able to explain a VLOOKUP to someone who's never built a formula before, without making them feel stupid, is a different skill — and it's the one that gets you remembered a decade later.</p>

<h2 id="whats-in-it">What We're Actually Going Through Together</h2>

<p>This isn't an abstract "Excel course" — it's built entirely around his actual job. The Session 1 practice dataset is 40 real-shaped purchase orders, with columns for PO Number, Order Date, Supplier, Category, Item Description, Quantity, Unit Price, Total Value, Status, Freight Cost, and GST Amount. Every formula tab teaches against that same data, so "SUM this column" always means something a procurement manager actually recognises.</p>

<p>A few examples of what we'll work through together:</p>

<p><strong>The foundations, done honestly.</strong> The workbook opens with a README that doesn't oversell Excel. It says, plainly: almost every business system on earth — an ERP, an accounting platform, a CRM, a bank statement export — ultimately stores its data as rows and columns, which is why Excel is the closest thing to a universal adapter for business data. It's just as honest about where Excel is weaker: once the job shifts from working out the answer to communicating that answer to someone else, a dashboard tool (or an AI-built app) usually serves the audience better than another spreadsheet. Knowing where that line sits is its own skill, and it's one I coach alongside Excel itself.</p>

<p><strong>Debugging real broken formulas, not toy examples.</strong> The Evaluate Formula tab walks through a genuine budget-check formula built on his data — <code>=IF(SUMIFS('Dataset 2'!K6:K155,'Dataset 2'!F6:F155,"IT Equipment")&gt;50000,"Over Budget","Within Budget")</code> — and shows, step by step, how Excel's own Evaluate Formula tool resolves the SUMIFS first, then the comparison, then the final IF result. Then there are 20 deliberately broken versions of formulas like it for him to find and fix himself.</p>

<p><strong>PivotTables taught as judgment, not just mechanics.</strong> Rather than handing over a pre-built pivot, the PivotTable tab is a guided build: click into Orders, drag Category into Rows, drag Total Value into Values, drag Status into Columns, format as currency, add Supplier as a filter. Then a real point I want him to internalise — two people given the same 300-row order table will build noticeably different pivots from it, because knowing which fields answer the actual question is judgment, not mechanics. Expect to rebuild it two or three times before it says what you actually want it to say.</p>

<p><strong>Exams that test the job, not just the syllabus.</strong> Each 50-question exam asks things like "What's the combined value of every order on this sheet?" and "How many orders are currently sitting at Pending?" — formula questions with a live dataset behind them, not multiple-choice trivia.</p>

<h2 id="practice-in-system">Why Practice-In-System Beats Watching a Tutorial</h2>

<p>Most of what's out there teaching Excel — YouTube tutorials, LinkedIn Learning courses, even a lot of paid training — follows the same pattern: someone demonstrates a formula in their own example, on their own data, and you watch. You might follow along in a blank sheet of your own if you're motivated, but nothing checks your work. You don't find out whether you actually understood <code>SUMIFS</code> until you're using it for real, usually under pressure, with no one there to tell you what went wrong or why.</p>

<p>This workbook is built to be attempted, not watched. Every practice question has a gold "type your own answer here" cell, and the Correct Formula and Correct Answer columns stay blank until you've actually typed something in — then they reveal, right next to a Check column that tells you immediately if you got it right. That's the whole mechanic: self-marking, automatically graded, instant feedback on every single formula. You're not reading about a formula. You're writing it yourself, submitting it, and finding out on the spot whether it's correct and why.</p>

<p>That difference matters more than it sounds. The Evaluate Formula tab takes it further — you're handed a genuinely broken formula and have to find the bug yourself using Excel's own Evaluate Formula tool, not watch someone else find it in a video. The Data Validation tab hands you live dropdowns and input limits to actually test, not a screenshot of one. It's the difference between reading about riding a bike and actually being on one (or, as a winger, between watching from the bench and actually getting a run).</p>

<h2 id="building-with-claude">Building It With Claude Instead of By Hand</h2>

<p>None of this was typed into Excel by hand. It was generated by a set of Python scripts that Claude wrote, ran, and refined — the whole file rebuilt from code, not assembled cell-by-cell in the Excel UI. The process ended up looking like this:</p>

<p><strong>1. Clarifying interview.</strong> Before building anything, Claude asked about quiz format, the shape of the PowerQuery dataset, and how much scaffolding a true beginner would need.</p>

<p><strong>2. Skeleton build.</strong> One script per major section — datasets, formula-teaching tabs, quizzes, Section 2 features, Section 3's relational tables and reporting tools.</p>

<p><strong>3. A shared style library.</strong> Fonts, colours, borders, and layout helpers — title bars, section headers, "worked example" and "your answer" cell styling — centralised in one place so every one of the 33 tabs looked consistent.</p>

<p><strong>4. A shared quiz engine.</strong> One reusable function built the "Your Answer → Correct Formula → Correct Answer → Check" mechanic used everywhere: every practice task, both Knowledge Checks, and all three exams. Built once, reused dozens of times.</p>

<p><strong>5. A recalculation quality gate.</strong> After every significant change, the whole workbook got rebuilt from scratch and run through a formula-recalculation tool. Every flagged error got individually inspected — most were harmless (question text that happened to contain the word "#N/A"), but several were real bugs: a broken cross-sheet reference, a <code>#DIV/0!</code> from a zero-match <code>AVERAGEIFS</code>, an unquoted sheet name with a space in it.</p>

<p><strong>6. Multiple full revision passes</strong>, each triggered by feedback — consolidating 16 separate formula tabs down to 6, rewriting multiple-choice questions to use safe dropdowns instead of free text, correcting the brand accent colour after the client flagged that the real logo uses green rather than gold (verified by sampling the actual logo file's pixels in Python, not eyeballing a screenshot; yes, the client had to tell me what colour my own logo is), and adding a full "Excel Fundamentals" tab after an audit turned up a gap in the foundations.</p>

<p>One bit I really like: when I needed to guarantee that no two "Simple Formulas" practice questions produced the same numeric answer, Claude reproduced the practice dataset in Python and computed every candidate answer to hand-pick combinations that were provably distinct. That's not a formatting trick — that's actual computation replacing what would otherwise be trial and error in Excel itself.</p>

<h2 id="the-real-advantage">The Real Advantage: One Skeleton, Infinite Bespoke Versions</h2>

<p>Here's the part most Excel coaching genuinely gets wrong. Making a course properly bespoke to a client's actual job — their data, their terminology, their day-to-day tools — takes real time by hand. Recolouring a title bar is nothing; rebuilding 33 tabs of formulas and practice datasets around a completely different dataset is not. So the sensible money move for most coaches is to build one generic workbook and reuse it for every client, regardless of whether they work in procurement, accounts payable, HR, or logistics. It's not laziness — it's just what manual spreadsheet-building makes viable at scale.</p>

<p>That's the constraint I don't have anymore. This workbook isn't really "an Excel course" — it's a skeleton: a shared style library, a shared self-marking quiz engine, a proven three-session structure, and a recalculation pipeline that catches errors before they ship. The only thing genuinely specific to procurement is the dataset and the wording around it — Purchase Orders, Suppliers, Freight Cost, GST. Swap that layer out for Accounts Payable data (Invoice Number, Vendor, Payment Terms, Due Date, Approval Status) or HR data (Employee ID, Department, Leave Type, Hours, Approval Status), and the same engine teaches the same core Excel skills through a lens that actually matches the next client's job.</p>

<h2 id="the-catch">The Catch: You Still Need to Know Excel</h2>

<p>Here's the part I don't want skipped over. None of this works if the person driving it doesn't actually know Excel. Claude wrote the scripts, generated the formulas, and rebuilt the file a dozen times in one sitting — but I still had to review every flagged error from the recalculation gate and judge which ones were real bugs versus false alarms. I still had to know that an <code>AVERAGEIFS</code> returning <code>#DIV/0!</code> on a zero-match condition is expected behaviour, not a formula written wrong. I still had to know what "good" looks like in a self-marking quiz mechanic, in a PivotTable, in a Power Query step — because Claude can build fast, but it can't tell you whether what it built is actually teaching the right thing. The same discipline applies every time I reskin this for a new client: I still have to check that the new dataset and formulas make sense for that industry, not just swap in new column headers and hope.</p>

<p>This is the same principle behind <a href="/blog/how-to-build-a-website-with-ai-no-code-under-100" style="color: #1f3a7d; text-decoration: underline; text-decoration-color: #00FF00; text-decoration-thickness: 2px; font-weight: 600;">building a website with AI</a>: the tool collapses the time it takes to produce something, it doesn't collapse the expertise needed to know if the output is right. If you can't read a formula, you can't catch the one formula in 2,126 that's subtly wrong. AI is a force multiplier on existing skill, not a replacement for it.</p>
